# Hearing loss and dementia — clinical context for OpenHear

**Status:** Research / clinical context note  
**Date:** 2026-08-19  
**Purpose:** Record the current evidence position so OpenHear does not overclaim, and so product language stays anchored in lived function rather than unproven cognitive-protection promises.

---

## What is well established

- There is a consistent **association** between hearing loss and higher rates of later dementia / cognitive decline in observational studies.
- Hearing loss is common in older adults; a large share of people who develop dementia will already have had hearing loss.
- Treating hearing loss improves communication, reduces listening effort, and can reduce social withdrawal. Those outcomes matter in their own right.

## What is not established

- **Causation is not proven.** Hearing loss could act as a cause, a catalyst, a consequence of shared neurodegenerative processes, or some combination.
- Major trial evidence (including the main ACHIEVE findings for the broader, healthier cohort) has **not** cleanly shown that hearing aids slow cognitive decline across the population studied.
- The 2024 *Lancet* Dementia Prevention, Intervention and Care update **reduced** the relative weight given to hearing loss compared with earlier reports; factors such as depression, diabetes, and social isolation rank higher in that analysis.
- UK professional bodies (BSA, BAA, BSHAA) have cautioned against public messaging that implies hearing loss *causes* dementia or that treating it will reliably reduce an individual’s dementia risk.

Popular and commercial messaging often collapses “association” into “cause” and “hearing aids prevent dementia.” That stronger claim is not supported by the current evidence base.

## Why this still matters for OpenHear

OpenHear’s justification does not depend on dementia-prevention claims.

The primary case rests on **lived function**:

- Speech-in-noise failure despite residual environmental hearing
- Loudness intolerance / recruitment — amplified sound becomes overwhelming, leading people to remove aids
- Sustained listening effort that ends in “switching off” or social avoidance
- Loss of confidence in work, family gatherings, and medical appointments when the acoustic scene is complex
- The long gap between first seeking help and still lacking usable support in everyday noise

These are sufficient reasons to build better personal, context-aware, and haptic-supported tools. They do not require an unproven causal story about dementia.

## How OpenHear should (and should not) speak about this

**Do**
- Emphasise reduced listening effort, better intelligibility in real environments, and restored participation.
- Treat social isolation and communication difficulty as first-class harms.
- Keep the Living Hearing Profile focused on what the person can and cannot do in the situations that actually matter to them.

**Do not**
- Claim that OpenHear, hearing aids, or haptic substitution prevent or delay dementia.
- Use dementia risk as the primary sales or advocacy argument.
- Present association studies as settled proof of causation.
